How Much Does a Kitchen Renovation Cost in Toronto in 2026?

A Toronto kitchen renovation can range from a focused cosmetic update to a full rebuild with a new layout. For early planning, broad 2026 market ranges start around $15,000 for a light refresh and can exceed $150,000 for a large, high-end or structurally complex project.

Kitchen Renovation Cost in Toronto: 2026 Planning Ranges

The first useful distinction is scope. Repainting existing cabinets and changing a backsplash is not the same project as removing the room to the studs, moving services and installing custom millwork. Kitchen size matters, but the work behind the finishes often creates the largest cost differences.

Important: These are broad 2026 Toronto and GTA market planning estimates, not Loyalty Contracting prices or quotes. Online guides vary substantially in what they include. Appliances, design, engineering, permits, taxes and unforeseen conditions may be treated differently. A project-specific site visit and written scope are needed for accurate pricing.

Toronto access, parking, elevator bookings, disposal arrangements and restricted working hours can also affect labour and logistics.

How Kitchen Size and Property Type Affect the Budget

Kitchen size affects the quantity of cabinetry, countertop, flooring and wall finish, but square footage alone does not predict cost. A small kitchen can still require a new panel circuit, difficult plumbing work or a large amount of custom cabinetry. A bigger room with a simple perimeter layout may be more straightforward to build.

Home type changes the logistics. In a condominium, the project may need to follow management rules for working hours, elevator reservations, contractor access, floor protection and waste removal. Shutoffs and penetrations can require coordination with building management. Homeowners should obtain the current renovation rules before design is finalized.

In a detached, semi-detached or row house, the age and construction of the building become important. Plaster walls, older electrical systems, limited service routes and shared or party-wall conditions can affect how work is designed and sequenced. Access for deliveries, bins and trades also varies from one Toronto property to another.

A useful estimate therefore describes the actual room and property rather than relying only on a cost per square foot. Measurements, service locations, access constraints and the condition of the surrounding finishes should all be reviewed.

What Makes a Kitchen Renovation More Expensive?

Cabinetry is often one of the largest items. Stock or semi-custom systems usually require less design and fabrication than fully custom millwork. The number of drawers, internal organizers, finished panels, tall cabinets and specialty details also matters.

Plumbing and electrical changes become more involved when a sink, dishwasher, range or major appliance moves. New circuits, panel capacity, venting and access to services may need to be addressed before finishes are installed.

Structural changes add design and construction work. Removing a wall is not just demolition if it supports loads or contains mechanical services. Engineering, temporary support, beams and permit documentation may become part of the scope.

Finish selections can shift the budget quickly. Countertop material and thickness, backsplash layout, flooring, hardware, plumbing fixtures, lighting and appliance specifications all affect material and installation costs.

Existing conditions are particularly important in older Toronto homes. Uneven floors, outdated wiring, concealed plumbing issues, water damage or walls that are not square can require corrective work before new finishes fit properly.

Where Does the Kitchen Renovation Budget Go?

There is no universal percentage breakdown that works for every kitchen. A project that reuses cabinets may spend more of its budget on counters and flooring, while a custom kitchen will allocate much more to millwork. A complete estimate commonly considers:

  • design, measurements and project coordination;
  • demolition, disposal and site protection;
  • cabinetry, panels, hardware and installation;
  • countertops and backsplash;
  • electrical circuits, lighting and devices;
  • plumbing rough-ins, sink, faucet and connections;
  • floor preparation and finished flooring;
  • drywall, trim, painting and finishing;
  • permits, design or engineering when required; and
  • appliances and related delivery or installation requirements.

When comparing quotations, look at inclusions and allowances rather than only the total. Confirm who supplies each item, whether disposal and protection are included, and how changes will be documented.

Does Changing the Kitchen Layout Increase the Cost?

Usually, yes. Keeping the sink, dishwasher and cooking location close to their existing services can reduce disruption. Moving them may require opening floors or walls, rerouting drains and water lines, adding electrical circuits, changing exhaust routes and repairing adjacent finishes.

An island can also carry more scope than its footprint suggests. A sink, dishwasher, receptacles or cooking appliance in an island may require services below the floor. In a finished basement or condo, access can be limited. The best time to test a new layout is during design, before cabinetry is ordered and demolition begins.

Do Toronto Kitchen Renovations Need a Permit?

It depends on the work. The City of Toronto lists cabinetry and millwork among examples that do not require a building permit. A permit may be required when the project includes structural or material alterations, adding or removing walls, new or enlarged openings, or changes to plumbing, drains or mechanical systems.

That means a cabinet-and-counter replacement can be treated differently from a kitchen that removes a wall or relocates services. Other approvals may also apply to specific work. Read our detailed guide to Toronto renovation permits, review the City’s current building-permit guidance, and confirm the requirements before construction.

How Long Can a Kitchen Renovation Take?

A responsible schedule starts before demolition. Design development, field measurements, selections, cabinetry production, permit review and long-lead materials can all occur before site work begins. Once construction starts, sequencing may include demolition, rough-ins, inspections, wall repairs, flooring, cabinetry, counters, backsplash, fixtures and final finishing.

A light refresh may involve fewer trades and less lead time than a complete rebuild. A large kitchen with custom cabinetry, structural work or delayed selections can take considerably longer. Treat any early duration as a planning estimate until the scope, materials and required approvals are settled.

How to Build a Realistic Kitchen Renovation Budget

  1. Define the problem first. Separate must-haves, such as better storage or safer electrical service, from optional style changes.
  2. Decide whether the layout stays. This choice influences cabinetry, services, permits and schedule.
  3. Choose appliances early. Their dimensions, power, gas, water and ventilation needs affect the design.
  4. Use comparable specifications. Ask each estimator to price the same cabinetry level, counter material and work scope.
  5. Allow for uncertainty. Older homes can reveal conditions that were not visible before demolition. Keep a separate contingency rather than spending the full budget on finishes.
  6. Review exclusions. Confirm taxes, appliances, design, permits, engineering, painting, disposal and temporary facilities.

Questions to Ask When Comparing Estimates

Ask whether the estimate is based on measured drawings and a written finish schedule. Confirm the cabinet construction and hardware allowance, countertop thickness and edge, number of electrical devices, extent of plumbing work, floor preparation, painting limits and disposal method. If a wall is changing, confirm whether structural review and related restoration are included.

Also ask how unknown conditions and owner-requested changes will be priced. A lower total can reflect missing scope rather than the same work at a better price. Clear assumptions make it easier to compare proposals and protect the budget once work is underway.
